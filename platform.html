About Platform Code

Platform

The limit on agent-written software is not how fast the code gets written. It is how much of it you can responsibly accept.

Most teams putting agents on a real codebase stall in the same place. The demos work. The throughput is real. And then nobody can say what was done, who agreed to it, or what would have caught it if it were wrong — so the work queues behind the few people trusted to read it, and the gain evaporates.

That is not a model problem, and another round of prompt tuning will not fix it. It is a question of what shape work is required to take, decided once and made to hold when nobody is watching.

establish facts → propose a plan → human decision → do the work → verify independently
01

Structure instead of supervision

Reviewing agent output case by case does not scale and is never consistent twice. When every change travels the same path, the question stops being “is this one good?” — asked fresh, forever — and becomes “did the process hold?”, which has an answer you can check. Consistency is what makes the volume survivable.

02

Human authority, moved earlier

The costly decision is approving the plan, not reacting to the result. Saying no is cheapest before the code exists, and a plan is small enough to actually read. People keep control of scope and of what is explicitly not being attempted, and spend their attention at the point where it still changes the outcome rather than at the point where it only creates rework.

03

Proof, not assurance

An agent reporting success is not evidence of anything. Work is checked by something that had no hand in producing it, and completion is confirmed against the system of record rather than a status someone wrote down. The output is a defensible account of what changed, who approved it, and what established that it was right — the thing an auditor, a regulator, or your own incident review will ask for.

Knowing when not to

Most changes need none of this. A single fix, a one-line config, anything you could describe in a paragraph — putting that through a structured pipeline buys nothing and costs real time and money. Judging where the structure earns its keep, and saying so plainly when it does not, is most of the work.
